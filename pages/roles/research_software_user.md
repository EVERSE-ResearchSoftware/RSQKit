## Introduction

A Research Software user in a very broad sense could be anyone who uses a piece of research software. However, though it is obviously important and necessary that software has all types of users, in this case we are talking more about those users who contribute to increasing the quality of research software by their experience of using it. Such users are, to use another term, often seen as **stakeholders** in the research software, and are in some cases included in any projects aimed at developing the research software. Users often drive development and therefore quality by reporting unexpected results, bugs and issues, and by influencing the priority levels for additions or enhancements to a current piece of research software.

A [Product Owner](./product_owner.md) is in this regard usually seen as the stakeholder-in-chief; they are responsible for collaborating with user stakeholders, identifying relevant items to be developed in the software, creating some kind of prioritisation of the issues, and ensuring that developers (such as [research software engineers](./research_software_engineer.md) or [researchers who code](./researcher_who_codes.md)) are then able to work on solving such issues, perhaps in conjunction with a [project manager](./project_manager.md).

A Research Software user is also tied into [what Research Software is](../research_software_and_quality/research_software.md); in the [three-tier model](../research_software_and_quality/three_tier_view.md) users become more common as the reach of the software grows through prototype tools and increased infrastructure. As the software grows, users more easily coalesce into a community and the shared experience can often drive further enhancements.

## Software development & quality responsibilities, challenges & tasks

The responsibilities here are similar to those of the Product Owner, who is focused on defining goals and prioritising the development of high-quality research software. The tasks a user could be required to perform include:

* Considering whether a piece of research software is reliable and produces results in a transparent manner.
* Providing requirements and ongoing feedback via focus/user groups and workshops to ensure the research software supports real workflows.
* Providing feedback on the user interface design and user experience of the research software.
* Translating research goals into actionable requirements — working with product owners and developers to convert objectives into implementable software tasks.
* Engaging with the development process used by the product owner and developers so that their voices are heard at opportune moments.
* Engage with the product owner and developers to produce user manuals and guides for the research software.
* Disseminate articles describing the research software, make presentations at conferences and other workshops with a view to initiate and grow a user community.

## Software development & quality guidance

* User-centred software design practices — ensuring software development is informed by real user needs and workflows. See for example the [ISO 9241-210 Human-centred design principles](https://www.iso.org/standard/77520.html) and the [UK Government Service Manual on user-centred design](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-centred-design).
* Agile and iterative development practices — supporting continuous improvement and adaptive planning through approaches such as [Agile software development](https://agilemanifesto.org/) and [Scrum](https://scrumguides.org/).
* Software sustainability principles — encouraging maintainable, reusable, and community-supported software. See guidance from the [Software Sustainability Institute](https://www.software.ac.uk/) and the [Research Software Alliance (ReSA)](https://www.researchsoft.org/).
* Research software usability and accessibility guidance — ensuring software is usable by diverse research communities, including consideration of the [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/) and inclusive design practices.
* Community governance models for research software — supporting transparent contribution and decision-making processes through practices such as contributor guidelines, codes of conduct, and open governance models. See guidance from [The Turing Way](https://book.the-turing-way.org/community-handbook/community-handbook.html) and the [Open Source Guides](https://opensource.guide/).
